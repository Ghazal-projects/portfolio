# Research Overview

[← Home](./)

I'm a PhD candidate at the [Kahlert School of Computing](https://www.cs.utah.edu/) at the University of Utah (since 2022), where I work with [Prof. Robert Ricci](https://ricci.io) as part of the [Flux Research Group](https://www.flux.utah.edu/). My research is about how computing systems can notice what is forming in their own data early enough to act on it, in a way people can follow. I call this Interpretable Signal-to-Control (O→S→C: observation, signal, control), and I have developed it in serverless computing, data center networking, and network security.

Most computing systems learn that something has happened only after it has happened. A container has gone cold. A link is congested. An attacker is already inside. Yet each of these builds momentum before it lands, and the data a system already produces usually carries traces of that momentum. My research starts from one question: can a system notice what is forming early enough to act on it, and do so in a way a person can follow?

The field has good answers to each half of that question, but rarely to both at once. Simple reactive rules are easy to trust, and they act late. Forecasting and learned policies act earlier, and often hide the reasoning behind their decisions. I work on the layer in between, and that is what O→S→C describes. Between what a system observes and what it does, I place an explicit signal: structure that is readable in data the system already emits, comes with a stated confidence, and maps onto an action an operator can inspect and audit.

I have built this layer in three areas that rarely cite one another. In **serverless computing**, WarmFlex reads how long a function runs and how soon it is likely to be called again, and keeps cold starts and idle containers steady together with rules that can be read line by line. In **data center networking**, my earlier work on flow scheduling (FMap, and flow-aware forwarding in SDN data centers) showed me that the real constraint sits before a flow can even be classified, so Combo forecasts how many large flows are coming before any single one reveals itself. In **network security**, NOD looks at attacker populations in SSH logs instead of single events, EPIC treats disagreement between independently trained learners as an early sign that a campaign is changing, and Vertex asks how a firewall can stay resilient once an attacker adapts to being detected.

Two things surprised me along the way. The first is that the signals that mattered took similar shapes in all three areas, even though the three communities share little vocabulary. That suggests the signal itself, more than any one system, is the thing worth studying. The second came from Combo: pushing a proactive idea as far as it would go, I ran into what I call the reactive wall, a point where the method simply stops helping. I reported it as a negative result, and mapping where an approach ends taught me more than the cases where it worked. I try to stay with a result until I understand what it actually is, including when it is not what I hoped for.

Looking ahead, two questions give my work its direction. One is transfer: if useful signals are shared across domains, a signal layer proven in one should carry to another with a loss that can be measured, so that how early a system can act becomes something we compute, not something we demonstrate case by case. The other is autonomy that knows its own limits: as systems and AI agents take on more decisions, they should judge the quality of their own perception, act while it holds, and hand the decision back when it does not. Two summer internships at the [AMANDA Lab](https://amanda.ece.utah.edu/people/) with [Prof. Massood Tabib-Azar](https://profiles.faculty.utah.edu/u0640514), where I ran machine-learning models on a very small chip inside a wearable biosensor, showed me that this kind of reasoning also has to fit where the decision is made.

What draws me most is pattern: the way structure emerges in data before the event it foreshadows. I find that interesting wherever it appears, and I am open to exploring it in new areas and with collaborators from any field.
